# Abolish Lawns — Design Context

## Design Context

### Users

Five entry points, one structural argument:

1. **Millennial HOA captive** — bought what they could afford, hates the lawn, lives under covenants. Needs permission and tactics.
2. **Zoomer city planner** — works for a broke municipality, knows the system is broken from the inside, wants policy ammunition.
3. **Boomer closet dissenter** — has always suspected the lawn is stupid, afraid to say so at the neighborhood association. Needs vindication with receipts.
4. **Conservative provocation target** — the HOA as government overreach, the weed ordinance as nanny state. Bait them in, radicalize them on land use.
5. **YIMBY/progressive planner** — needs policy briefs, model ordinances, case studies, citable data.

Context of use: desktop reading (long-form policy content, printable PDFs) and mobile browsing (manifesto, social sharing). Users arrive angry, skeptical, or curious — they should leave convinced.

### Brand Personality

Three words: **furious, rigorous, dry**.

This is not a gardening blog. It is not an environmental nonprofit. It is a political document dressed in the clothes of a policy journal. The argument is polemical but the evidence is airtight. The tone does not beg — it indicts.

- Voice: The International Encyclopedia of Urban Planning crossed with a leaflet slipped under a windshield wiper.
- Register: Clinical. The numbers are damning; let them be damning. No exclamation points. No hope-speak.
- Emotional goal: The reader should feel the slow burn of recognition — "I always knew this was stupid and now I can prove it."

### Aesthetic Direction

**"Policy brief from hell"** — clinical precision weaponized as polemic.

- Think: A UN environmental report formatted by someone who also makes protest zines. Government data. Radical conclusions. Perfect kerning.
- Anti-references: Cozy gardening aesthetics (warm greens, botanical illustration, Pollan-esque). Generic nonprofit teal-and-hope. Tech startup rounded corners and Inter. Dry PDF-brained government gray.
- Color: Both light and dark modes (system preference). The palette should feel like something printed — high contrast, not soft. Think newsprint black, not charcoal. A single sharp accent — acid green (the color of a lawn you're about to destroy) or a hard institutional yellow.
- Typography: The physical object is a 1970s government technical report with handwritten margin notes. A condensed grotesque or an older sans-serif with institutional weight for display; something with no warmth and maximum authority. Body copy that is legible at 2am in a bad mood.
- NOT Inter. NOT Fraunces. NOT any of the reflex fonts.
- Images: Data visualizations, maps, satellite imagery of lawns, before/after land conversions. No stock photography of smiling people holding plants.

### Design Principles

1. **Every aesthetic choice is an argument.** The design should feel like it was made by someone who read the data and got angry, not by someone who wanted the site to be pretty.
2. **Density is a virtue.** This is a policy document. Do not pad. Do not breathe. The Millennial in the HOA reads dense things; the zoomer planner reads denser ones.
3. **Authority before warmth.** The site should feel citable. If a user wants to quote it, it should feel like quoting a report, not a blog post.
4. **Entry points are real.** Different audiences land at different pages. Each page should be self-contained enough to convert a first-time reader with no prior context.
5. **Printability is a feature.** PDFs and zines are first-class outputs. The design system must accommodate print styles.
